# Attention Science for a Hands-Busy Activity While Watching Narrative Video (Anime)

Scope note: this covers peer-reviewed evidence on fidgeting, dual-task interference, motor automaticity, media multitasking and narrative transportation, haptic/eyes-free interaction, curiosity and reward capture, and optimal arousal in ADHD. Most findings come from abstracts, publisher pages, PMC pages and secondary summaries found by search. Full texts were mostly not read (www.psy.fsu.edu was blocked by the session's network settings, so the Sarver 2015 PDF could not be opened). Where I could not verify effect sizes, I say so.

Evidence strength key: **Strong** = replicated, multiple labs, or a meta-analysis / well-established theory. **Moderate** = one or two decent experiments. **Weak** = small, correlational, exploratory, or press-level only.

## 1. Fidgeting and attention: does it help or hurt?

### Takeaway
The evidence is mixed and depends on who is fidgeting and what kind of movement it is. Spontaneous, low-demand movement that people generate themselves (ankle or gross-motor activity, doodling during a boring audio task) is linked to equal or better performance, especially in ADHD. An external "toy" that draws visual attention (a fidget spinner) made attention worse in young children with ADHD. In typical adults, fidgeting is mostly a marker of mind-wandering, not a fix for it.

### Cited Findings
- **Hartanto, Krafft, Iosif & Schweitzer (2016), *Child Neuropsychology* 22(5):618–626.** N = 44 children aged 10–17 (26 ADHD, 18 typically developing). They wore an ankle actometer during a flanker (cognitive control) task, and movement was analysed trial by trial. More intense movement went with *better* accuracy in ADHD but not in typically developing children. ADHD children moved more intensely on correct trials than on error trials. The authors read this as movement used to self-regulate alertness (making up for under-arousal). **Weak–moderate:** small sample, correlational, so it does not show that movement causes better performance. — [PMC4675699](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4675699); [eScholarship PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt85w054gt/qt85w054gt.pdf?t=rtw7kx)
- Popular coverage (e.g., CHADD, Psychology Today) went beyond the paper, for example by calling for movement to be encouraged as a priority. The study did not test that claim. — [CHADD "Don't stop the movement"](https://chadd.org/attention-article/dont-stop-the-movement/)
- **Sarver, Rapport, Kofler, Raiker & Friedman (2015), *J Abnormal Child Psychology* 43(7):1219–1232**, "Hyperactivity in ADHD: Impairing deficit or compensatory behavior?" It tests the functional working-memory model (Rapport et al. 2009), which says gross-motor hyperactivity in ADHD rises with working-memory demand and may make up for prefrontal under-activation. Later papers cite it as evidence that working memory is linked to objectively measured hyperactivity in ADHD. I could not open the full text. The commonly reported result is that ADHD children who moved more performed better on phonological working-memory tasks, while movement did not help, or slightly hurt, typically developing children. **That result is unverified in this pass.** — [Springer PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10802-015-0011-1.pdf); [UCF record](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2015/266)
- **Graziano, Garcia & Landis (2018/2020), *Journal of Attention Disorders*.** N = 60 preschoolers with ADHD (mean age about 4.9). A-B-A-B design inside a classroom intervention, with accelerometers plus 5-minute videotaped attention coding. Spinners lowered activity levels in the first phase only, and went with *lower* attention in both phases. Conclusion: spinners hurt attention in young children with ADHD. **Moderate**, but the sample is very young children, and the authors say the results may not apply to older children or people without ADHD. — [PubMed 29676193](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29676193/); [FIU PDF](https://self-regulationlab.fiu.edu/fidgetarticlegraziano.pdf); [BrainFacts Q&A](https://www.brainfacts.org/diseases-and-disorders/childhood-disorders/2018/do-fidget-spinners-help-kids-focus-071818)
- **Aspiranti & Hulac (2019) review:** fidget spinners may reduce some disruptive behaviour but did not improve attention. — summarised in [PMC9120292](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9120292)
- **Andrade (2010), "What does doodling do?" *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 24(1):100–106, DOI 10.1002/acp.1561.** N = 40 people listening to a dull mock phone message. Those who shaded shapes while listening recalled about 29% more on a surprise memory test. Proposed mechanism: doodling uses just enough resources to stop daydreaming without taking much from listening. Neither daydreaming nor arousal was measured. Note that the primary task was **audio only**; doodling did not compete with any visual input. **Weak–moderate:** small and single-study, with mixed replications. Chan (2012) found doodling *hurt* memory for visual material, and unstructured doodling has done worse than structured doodling or note-taking. — [Simply Psychology summary](https://www.simplypsychology.org/andrade-doodling.html); [Plymouth record](https://pearl.plymouth.ac.uk/handle/10026.1/4701?show=full); [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/what-does-doodle-do-it-boosts-your-memory-and-concentration)
- **Carriere, Seli & Smilek (2013), *Canadian J Experimental Psychology*, "Wandering in both mind and body."** Four studies using self-report scales. Fidgeting was uniquely predicted by inattention and *spontaneous* (unintentional) mind-wandering, not by deliberate mind-wandering. **Moderate for the link; self-report only.** — [Duke Scholars](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/1330890)
- **Farley, Risko & Kingstone (2013), *Frontiers in Psychology* 4:619.** Fidgeting during a video lecture was coded from video. Attention and retention both fell with time on task. Fidgeting predicted *lower* retention even after controlling for attention, and the authors suggest a stress-based account. **Weak–moderate, correlational.** — [PMC3776418](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3776418)
- **Fine-motor activity in ADHD students (2009, DOI 10.1080/19404150903524515; likely Kercood & Grskovic, authors not confirmed in search).** Two exploratory studies. A fine-motor activity went with more maths problems correct in a high-stimulation classroom analogue and reduced the effect of auditory distraction. The authors call the effects "modest." **Weak.** — [Taylor & Francis abstract](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/19404150903524515)
- UC Davis researchers say self-generated ("intrinsic") fidgeting such as hair twirling, pen clicking or rocking may help people with ADHD focus. They are still testing whether a device (a "smart" fidget ball) can give the same benefit. This is expert commentary, not a result. — [UC Davis Health, Oct 2024](https://health.ucdavis.edu/neurology/news/headlines/does-fidgeting-help-people-with-adhd-focus-/2024/10)

### Inferences
- What helps ADHD attention seems to be **self-paced, low-demand movement that the person does not look at** (ankle activity, idle squeezing). Novel objects that are interesting to watch, like a spinning toy, pull attention away. A design should aim to feel like "fidgeting" rather than "playing a toy."
- Andrade's doodling benefit came from an *auditory* primary task with little else to attend to. Anime with subtitles is visually demanding, so a visual doodle-style task would likely *compete* (see Chan 2012 on visual memory). The transferable idea is "a just-sufficient load that stops mind-wandering," not "doodling" itself.
- For typical (non-ADHD) viewers, fidgeting tends to be a sign that the content has stopped holding attention. The activity may help most in slow or boring stretches and matter little during gripping scenes.

### Gaps
- I found no study of fidgeting or doodling during *video or film* narrative viewing with emotion or immersion as the outcome. All evidence comes from lectures, classroom tasks, audio or lab cognition tasks.
- Sarver 2015 effect sizes and the low-load vs high-load breakdown could not be verified (host blocked).
- Adult ADHD evidence is thin. Most of these studies involve children.

## 2. Dual-task interference and multiple resource theory (Wickens)

### Takeaway
Multiple Resource Theory predicts that two tasks clash least when they use different modalities (visual vs auditory), different processing codes (spatial vs verbal), different visual channels (focal vs peripheral) and different stages (perception/cognition vs response). Subtitled anime places heavy load on **focal vision** and **verbal processing**. The least conflicting add-on is therefore a **manual, spatial-motor, eyes-free task that needs no reading, no focal vision and few decisions**. Even then, some cost remains, because executive control is shared.

### Cited Findings
- **Wickens (2008), "Multiple Resources and Mental Workload," *Human Factors* 50(3):449–455, DOI 10.1518/001872008X288394.** The model has four two-way dimensions: processing **stages** (perception/cognition vs response), perceptual **modalities** (auditory vs visual), **visual channels** (focal vs ambient/peripheral) and processing **codes** (spatial vs verbal). Together they account for differences in how well tasks time-share. Tasks that differ on these dimensions time-share better. Wickens stresses that using different resources does **not** guarantee perfect time-sharing. **Strong (long-established theory, widely applied).** — [Wickens 2008 PDF](https://interruptions.net/literature/Wickens-HF08.pdf); [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1518%2F001872008X288394)
- In the computational form used in driving research, each pair of tasks gets a conflict value. Two tasks that share no resources get 0, but a default cost of about 0.2 applies just for doing two things at once. — [Iowa Driving Assessment paper](https://pubs.lib.uiowa.edu/driving/article/28160/galley/136452/view); [Wickens 2002, "Multiple resources and performance prediction"](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/multiple-resources-and-performance-prediction/812454061046497281-13452)
- **Lang's Limited Capacity Model of Motivated Mediated Message Processing (LC4MP; Lang 2000, *J Communication* 50:46–70).** Viewers have limited capacity for encoding, storage and retrieval. Secondary-task reaction-time (STRT) probes during TV viewing slow down when the content needs more resources. Basil found that content features (scene changes, number of people) slowed STRTs more than formal features (music, camera motion). There is debate over what STRTs actually measure (Lang & Basil 1998). **Strong as a framework; measurement is debated.** — [LC4MP overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LC4MP); [Lang et al. 2006, "Parsing the Resource Pie" (Media Psychology)](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Annie-Lang-2/publication/228645194_Parsing_the_Resource_Pie_Using_STRTs_to_Measure_Attention_to_Mediated_Messages/links/00b7d528393a870648000000/Parsing-the-Resource-Pie-Using-STRTs-to-Measure-Attention-to-Mediated-Messages.pdf); [Basil, message processing](https://people.uleth.ca/~michael.basil/message.html)
- **Subtitles are a load in their own right.** Kruger and colleagues found that viewer immersion and visual attention vary considerably by scene type (Kruger et al. 2016). Same-language subtitles *raised* transportation for second-language viewers. — [Kruger & Doherty ARSAD 2017 slides](https://webs.uab.cat/arsad/wp-content/uploads/sites/251/2017/03/kruger_arsad_2017.pdf). In an earlier Comunicar study, subtitles did not reduce presence or enjoyment; first language and viewing habits explained most of the variance in transportation. — [Comunicar preprint](https://revistacomunicar.com/pdf/preprint/50/c5002en.pdf)
- **Szarkowska, Kruger et al. (2024, *PLOS ONE*):** watching subtitled video **with the sound off** raised cognitive load and lowered comprehension, immersion and enjoyment. This shows the audio track carries real narrative load. Losing a channel, or giving it to another task, costs immersion. — [UEA record](https://research-portal.uea.ac.uk/en/publications/watching-subtitled-videos-with-the-sound-off-affects-viewers-comp/); [PDF](https://ueaeprints.uea.ac.uk/96946/1/PLOSOne_2024_e0306251.pdf)
- **Holmes et al. (2009, *PLoS ONE*) and Holmes et al. (2010).** Playing Tetris (a visuospatial task) *after* a trauma film reduced intrusive flashbacks over one week, while recognition memory for the film was unaffected. The rationale is that visuospatial tasks compete for the resources needed to form vivid mental images. A verbal task (Pub Quiz) did not reduce flashbacks and even increased them in one experiment. **Moderate (replicated lab analogue).** — [PMC2607539](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2607539); [PMC2978094](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2978094)

### Inferences
- Anime with subtitles uses: **visual-focal** (subtitles, faces), **verbal** (dialogue and subtitles), **auditory** (voice acting, music) and **cognitive** (story model, emotional appraisal). The free resources are mainly **manual response**, **tactile/proprioceptive input** and, partly, **peripheral vision**.
- The best fit is therefore a task that is (a) operated by touch and proprioception, (b) has no text, numbers or verbal labels, (c) needs no focal glances, and (d) needs no decisions. Repetitive rhythmic actions (rolling, squeezing, clicking, tracing a fixed shape) suit this.
- **Avoid visuospatial-imagery load.** By the Holmes logic, a task that uses visuospatial working memory (Tetris-like placement, spatial puzzles) may dull the vivid imagery that carries emotional scenes, even if plot recall survives. That is a direct threat to "emotional immersion" as opposed to comprehension.
- Avoid **verbal** load as well (counting, words, reading on-device), because it clashes with dialogue and subtitles.
- Even an "orthogonal" task carries a basic concurrence cost (about 0.2 in the computational model). The goal is to reduce cost, not to reach zero.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests *manual-only* tasks during *subtitled* narrative video with immersion measured. The predictions come from theory.
- The Holmes paradigm imposed the task *after* viewing. During-viewing visuospatial tasks would likely affect encoding too, but this is extrapolation.

## 3. Motor automaticity and procedural memory

### Takeaway
Well-practised motor skills need far less attention. Experts even perform *better* when distracted and *worse* when they attend to their movements. But automaticity takes practice, and it breaks down when the task brings novelty, decisions, errors to fix, or moments where the person consciously monitors the movement. A watch-along hand activity should be learnable to automaticity quickly, and should not keep making the person re-attend.

### Cited Findings
- **Beilock et al. (2004, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*) and the broader golf-putting work.** Novices putted more accurately when attending to their movement than under a dual task. Experts showed the reverse: better under a dual task, worse when skill-focused. Proceduralised skills "benefit from environments that limit, rather than encourage, attention to execution." **Strong (replicated across tasks).** — [Beilock et al., "Choking under pressure" PB&R PDF](https://homepage.villanova.edu/diego.fernandezduque/Teaching/CognitivePsychology/Lectures_and_Labs/s4ExecutiveFunctions/sReading/ChokeUnderPressurePB&R.pdf)
- Dual-task interference is the standard index of automaticity: an automatic task is much less affected by a concurrent task. Experts need fewer resources for a trained motor task. — [PMC8107505](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8107505); [Saarland 2022 paper on expertise and dual-tasking](https://jahrbib.sulb.uni-saarland.de/bitstream/20.500.11880/32719/1/1-s2.0-S000169182200018X-main.pdf)
- Caveat: skill level and automaticity develop together with practice and are hard to separate. Some studies even find dual-task *benefits*, so "automaticity" as an explanation needs care. — [Frontiers editorial, cognitive-motor interference, PMC6682639](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6682639/)
- Pressure or worry acts like a dual task: worries compete for the working memory a skill needs. — [Beilock PB&R PDF](https://homepage.villanova.edu/diego.fernandezduque/Teaching/CognitivePsychology/Lectures_and_Labs/s4ExecutiveFunctions/sReading/ChokeUnderPressurePB&R.pdf)

### Inferences
- **What breaks automaticity** (from the literature above, plus the standard skill-acquisition framework):
  1. *Attention to execution* (explicit monitoring: "am I doing it right?"). A score, accuracy target or failure state invites this.
  2. *Decision points* (choosing between options). Each choice uses central/cognitive resources that MRT says are shared with story comprehension.
  3. *Error detection and correction*. A task that can "go wrong" and needs fixing pulls focal attention back.
  4. *Novelty or changing rules*. New patterns reset the learner to an early, attention-heavy stage.
  5. *Stakes or pressure* (losing progress), which act as a worry-based dual task.
- Design implication: the motion should be simple, constant and rhythmic, with no fail state and no need for precision. Any changes should be ones the hands can absorb without conscious appraisal.

### Gaps
- **How long until a simple motor task becomes automatic.** I did not retrieve a citable source in this pass. The general picture in skill-acquisition research is a power-law speed-up with practice, with simple repetitive movements becoming low-demand within minutes to hours and complex skills taking much longer. Specific numbers need a source before use (e.g., Fitts & Posner 1967 stages; Newell & Rosenbloom 1981 power law; Logan 1988 instance theory).
- No data on how much attention "idle" manual stereotypies (pen clicking, knitting stitches for experienced knitters) take during video viewing.

## 4. Media multitasking, narrative transportation, emotion and comprehension

### Takeaway
Doing a *media* task (tweeting, second-screen browsing) while watching consistently lowers transportation, emotional intensity, enjoyment and recall. Transportation appears to be the step through which emotion and enjoyment are lost. I found no direct study showing that a *low-demand, non-media manual* secondary task leaves enjoyment intact. That remains a theory-based hope, not a finding.

### Cited Findings
- **Green & Brock's transportation construct:** immersion in a story through focused attention, emotional engagement and mental imagery. Focused attention is part of the definition itself. — [Transportation theory overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation_theory_(psychology))
- **Park, Xu, Rourke & Bellur (2019), *J Broadcasting & Electronic Media*, "Do you enjoy TV while tweeting?"** Tweeting while watching a sitcom lowered transportation. Lower transportation weakened emotional responses, which in turn lowered enjoyment (a mediation chain). The largest effect was on transportation. **Moderate**, single experiment; sample and effect sizes not verified (abstract and press only). — [DOI 10.1080/08838151.2019.1622340](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/08838151.2019.1622340); [Butler repository](https://digitalcommons.butler.edu/ccom_papers/263); [Phys.org summary](https://phys.org/news/2019-07-tweeting-tv-diminishes-enjoyment.pdf)
- **Van Cauwenberge, Schaap & Van Roy (2014), *Computers in Human Behavior*.** N = 85. Second-screen use during TV news lowered factual recall and comprehension, and cognitive load accounted for the drop. Whether the second-screen content was relevant or irrelevant made no significant difference. In other words, load matters more than whether the topic is related. **Moderate.** — [dblp record](https://dblp.uni-trier.de/rec/journals/chb/CauwenbergeSR14.html); [KU Leuven](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/318315)
- **Schaap, Kleemans & Van Cauwenberge (2018), *CHB*.** N = 121. Second screening lowered factual recognition **and program liking**, whatever the presentation mode. **Moderate.** — [UvA PDF](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/12519729/06.pdf)
- **Adolescent diary study (Xu et al.):** media multitasking made adolescents feel both better and worse about their main activity. This was a different setting, not TV narrative. — [PMC8570485](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8570485); [AIP Inside Science](https://www.aip.org/inside-science/media-multitasking-gives-us-mixed-feelings)
- A 2016 multimedia-narrative experiment found transportation and emotional reactions did not depend on presentation modality, but knowledge gain was slightly lower with multimodal presentation. — [Sage references page](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/ref/10.1177/1077699016654679)
- The subtitles study (Section 2) found presence and enjoyment were *not* reduced by subtitles, even though they divide visual attention. Viewers can adapt to a habitual extra load. — [Comunicar preprint](https://revistacomunicar.com/pdf/preprint/50/c5002en.pdf)

### Inferences
- All the negative findings involve **secondary tasks that are verbal, visual and attention-grabbing** (tweets, browsing, reading), which are exactly the channels anime uses. They don't directly test a tactile/manual fidget task, but they show the mechanism: anything that lowers *focused attention* lowers transportation, and emotion and enjoyment follow.
- Van Cauwenberge's "relevant vs irrelevant made no difference" suggests that making the hand activity *thematically* tied to the anime will not protect comprehension if it adds load.
- The subtitle-habituation result hints that a *habitual*, low-load extra activity can be absorbed without hurting enjoyment. This is indirect support for an automatised hand task.
- For ADHD viewers whose baseline problem is under-arousal or mind-wandering (Sections 1 and 7), a very low-load task might *raise* net transportation by stopping drift. This is plausible but untested for video.

### Gaps
- **No peer-reviewed study found that tests a low-demand non-media manual task (knitting, fidget, stress ball) during narrative film/TV with transportation or emotion as outcomes.** This is the single most important gap for the design.
- Effect sizes for Park et al. 2019 were not retrieved.
- I found no anime-specific or subtitled-fiction multitasking studies.

## 5. Haptic/tactile feedback and the need for visual monitoring

### Takeaway
Fairly consistent HCI evidence, mostly from driving: adding haptic or tactile feedback, or physical landmarks, to a control reduces how long and how often people must look at it. Effects range from about 19% to 39% less eyes-off-road time. Static physical structure (ridges, bumps, detents) is especially effective. Some mid-air or extra-feedback setups backfire on certain tasks.

### Cited Findings
- **Pitts et al. (automotive touchscreens):** visual plus haptic feedback cut total eyes-off-road time by **39%** (button tasks) and **19%** (slider tasks), with negligible change in task performance. **Moderate (simulator).** — reported in search results among [NHTSA/ROSAP haptic touchscreen reports](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/26086) and [CORE](https://core.ac.uk/download/84340656.pdf) (exact host of the Pitts paper not confirmed)
- **Northwestern/Ford simulator study (haptic touchscreen, surface-friction display):** haptic feedback significantly reduced eyes-off-road time and perceived workload, compared with visual-only. — [Northwestern CCITT final report](https://peshkin.mech.northwestern.edu/publications/CCITT_Final_Report_Y501.pdf); [Mullenbach thesis](https://robotics.northwestern.edu/documents/publications/Mullenbach_Masters_Thesis_Final.pdf)
- **Harrington et al. (AutoUI 2018), mid-air ultrasound haptics:** gestures with haptics reduced long glances and mean off-road glance time. But for button selection, haptics *increased* visual demand. — [Nottingham PDF](https://nottingham-repository.worktribe.com/preview/1300318/Harrington-Mid-Air%20Haptics-AutoUI2018.pdf)
- **HaptiCase (Corsten et al. 2015, RWTH):** tactile landmarks (dot grids) on the back of a phone significantly improved **eyes-free** tapping accuracy, and had no effect when users could look. — [RWTH PDF](https://hci.rwth-aachen.de/publications/corsten2015a.pdf)
- **LMU (Rümelin et al., TEI 2014):** static haptic structures support users well, and controls that come to the finger reduce visual attention. — [LMU PDF](https://www.mmi.ifi.lmu.de/pubdb/publications/pub/ruemelin2014tei/ruemelin2014tei.pdf)
- **Counter-example (QMUL preliminary study):** adding tactile feedback to audio *slowed* eyes-free touchscreen menu navigation. The authors call it a limited preliminary study. — [QMUL news 2014](https://www.qmul.ac.uk/news/latest-news/2014/se/how-could-smartphones-be-easier-to-use-when-we-cant-look-at-the-screen.html)

### Inferences
- A hand activity with **physical, tactile structure** (detents, textures, clicks, distinct shapes) can be done without looking. A flat touchscreen game needs glances, and every glance takes focal vision away from subtitles and faces.
- Haptics work best as *confirmation* of a simple action (a "click" felt), not as an information channel to decode. Decoding vibration patterns would add a cognitive or perceptual load.
- Smartphone-based designs should rely on large, location-free gestures (swipe anywhere, squeeze, tilt) plus haptic ticks, rather than targets that must be hit.

### Gaps
- Most data are from driving simulators; no study found of haptics during leisure video viewing.
- No quantified data on whether game-controller rumble specifically reduces glances in a non-driving context.

## 6. Curiosity and reward anticipation capturing attention

### Takeaway
Strong evidence that (a) stimuli previously linked to reward capture attention involuntarily, even when irrelevant, and (b) unresolved "information gaps" (wanting to know an outcome) engage reward circuits and pull attention toward resolving them. This matches the user's report that outcome-curiosity breaks immersion. A hand activity should have **no uncertain outcomes, no variable rewards and no reveals**.

### Cited Findings
- **Anderson, Laurent & Yantis (2011), "Value-driven attentional capture," *PNAS* 108(25):10367–10371.** After reward learning, previously rewarded colours captured attention even when they were inconspicuous and irrelevant to the task. This is a value-based capture mechanism, separate from capture by salience or by goals. **Strong (heavily replicated paradigm).** — [PMC3121816](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3121816); [PNAS PDF](https://andersonlab.sites.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/36/2016/09/PNAS_2011.pdf)
- **Anderson, Laurent & Yantis (2011), *PLOS ONE*:** learned value magnifies capture by salient stimuli, and the boost fades only over several hundred unrewarded trials. — [PLOS ONE PDF](https://andersonlab.sites.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/36/2016/09/PLOS_2011.pdf)
- **Loewenstein (1994) information-gap theory:** curiosity is the felt gap between what one knows and wants to know. It is strongest when partly informed. Golman & Loewenstein model attention as central: the gap affects utility *while one is thinking about it*. — [Golman & Loewenstein PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf); [Information-Gap Theory 2016](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/Information-Gap%20Theory%202016.pdf)
- **Kang et al. (2009, *Psychological Science*), "The wick in the candle of learning":** high curiosity activated caudate and reward regions before the answer was revealed. People paid in time or tokens to learn answers, and curiosity peaked at moderate confidence. These points come from secondary descriptions; I did not read the primary text. — [CMU PDF of Kang et al.](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/WickCandleLearning.pdf); [Columbia review PDF](https://shohamylab.zuckermaninstitute.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/2017-02/Curiosity.pdf)
- People will give up reward to get advance information even when it does not change the outcome (non-instrumental information seeking). — [Golman, Hagmann & Loewenstein, "Information avoidance"](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/the-demand-for__and-avoidance-of__information.pdf)

### Inferences
- The anime itself is meant to be the curiosity engine (plot information gaps). A hand activity that creates its *own* information gaps (random drops, gacha-like outcomes, "what will I get?", score reveals, progress bars nearing a threshold) sets up a competing gap. Value-driven capture says the cue will grab attention automatically.
- Even small learned-reward cues (a colour or sound linked to a "win") can keep capturing attention for hundreds of exposures. So "it's just a little reward" is not harmless.
- A safer feedback model is **deterministic, continuous and outcome-less** sensory feedback (a click each time, a texture), which gives arousal without uncertainty.

### Gaps
- No study found that directly tests reward or curiosity mechanics in a secondary task and their effect on narrative immersion. The inference rests on basic attention research.

## 7. Boredom, under-stimulation and optimal arousal in ADHD

### Takeaway
The Moderate Brain Arousal (MBA) model, with supporting experiments, says people with ADHD need *more* external stimulation (noise) to reach optimal performance, while the same stimulation hurts controls. This supports a low-level, constant, non-informative stimulus (like a steady tactile rhythm) for ADHD viewers, and warns that it may slightly hurt non-ADHD viewers.

### Cited Findings
- **Sikström & Söderlund (2007), *Psychological Review*:** the MBA model says moderate arousal gives the best performance (inverted U). Through stochastic resonance, a hypodopaminergic (ADHD) brain needs more external noise to reach that optimum. **Moderate (theory with some support).** — [GU PDF](https://www.gu.se/sites/default/files/2022-11/GS%20Psych%20Rev.pdf)
- **Söderlund, Sikström & Smart (2007), "Listen to the noise," *J Child Psychol Psychiatry* 48(8):840–847:** white noise *improved* cognitive (memory) performance in the ADHD group and *worsened* it in controls. **Moderate (small samples, later replications mixed).** — [PubMed 17683456](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17683456/)
- **Söderlund et al. (2010):** background white noise moderated performance in non-clinical inattentive participants too. — [PMC2955636](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2955636)
- Hartanto 2016 (Section 1) proposes the same mechanism for movement: hyperactivity as self-regulation against under-arousal. — [PMC4675699](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4675699)
- Farley et al. 2013 and Carriere et al. 2013 (Section 1): mind-wandering and fidgeting rise with time on task and boredom. — [PMC3776418](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3776418)

### Inferences
- For an ADHD viewer, a hand activity can serve as the "noise" that tops up arousal. It should be **constant, low-information and self-paced**, never attention-demanding or reward-signalling.
- Because MBA predicts stimulation *hurts* those already at optimal arousal, the activity should be **optional and adjustable in intensity**. Users can then ramp it up in slow scenes and drop it during tense or emotional ones, when the content supplies enough arousal.
- An adaptive idea: arousal needs change scene by scene. Kruger found immersion and attention vary by scene type, and LC4MP shows resource demand tracks content. So "hands slow down when the story heats up" fits the theory.

### Gaps
- MBA tests mostly use auditory noise and memory tasks, not tactile stimulation during video.
- Later meta-analyses of white noise in ADHD (not retrieved here) are reported to show small, heterogeneous effects. They should be checked before strong claims.

## Cross-cutting design implications (inferences, not findings)
1. **Channel:** manual plus tactile/proprioceptive only. No text, numbers or verbal content; no focal visual targets; no visuospatial puzzle-solving (Wickens MRT; Holmes Tetris; subtitle load).
2. **Load:** just enough to stop drift (Andrade; MBA) but automatised quickly. Simple, rhythmic, repetitive actions (Beilock automaticity).
3. **No breaks of automaticity:** no fail states, precision demands, branching decisions or rule changes.
4. **No curiosity or reward hooks:** deterministic feedback only. No random rewards, reveals or score thresholds (Anderson value-driven capture; information-gap theory).
5. **Physical tactile structure** so the task can be done without looking (haptic HCI: 19–39% fewer glances).
6. **Optional and adaptive intensity:** helps under-aroused (ADHD/boring scenes) viewers, may slightly hurt others (Söderlund noise; Graziano spinners).
7. **Biggest evidence gap:** no direct experiment tests a low-demand manual task's effect on transportation or emotion during narrative video. Everything above is extrapolated from adjacent research.
